# Video Scripting as a Science

**A repeatable philosophy for scripts people finish and feel. Working name: the Attention Ledger.**

Synthesis of how the best video creators alive actually script (MrBeast's leaked internal guide, Jenny Hoyos, Derek Muller, Johnny Harris, Kurzgesagt, Ryan Trahan, George Blackman, Paddy Galloway, Alex Hormozi) cross-checked against the psychology and neuroscience that explains why it works. Compiled July 2026.

---

## The claim

Scripting gets treated as art. The evidence says most of it is engineering. The top creators converge on the same mechanics independently, and those mechanics match what curiosity research, narrative neuroscience, and platform retention data predict.

The philosophy in one line: **a video is a loan of attention, and the script is the repayment schedule.** The title and thumbnail take out the loan. Every open loop borrows more. Every payoff repays some. Boredom is default on the debt. The final second settles the account and decides how the viewer remembers you.

Treat this as literal accounting you can audit line by line. Every sentence in a script either takes out attention credit, services it, or wastes it. The rest of this document is the evidence, the laws it implies, and the process that makes it repeatable.

---

## 1. The evidence layer

Five findings do most of the work.

**Curiosity is a debt instrument.** George Loewenstein's information gap theory (1994) defines curiosity as a deprivation state: a felt gap between what you know and what you want to know, functioning like hunger. Two implications matter for scripts. A small dose of information primes curiosity, so hooks must give partial information rather than withhold everything. And gaps feel most urgent when the picture is nearly complete, so a script that keeps the viewer at "almost understanding" compounds curiosity as it goes.

**Open loops occupy the mind.** The Zeigarnik effect: unresolved tasks stay active in memory while completed ones fade. A specific unanswered question planted at second 5 keeps working at minute 5. This is the mechanism behind foreshadowing, cold opens, and "at the end I'll show you what happened."

**Stories synchronize brains, literally.** Uri Hasson's Princeton lab put speakers and listeners in fMRI during natural storytelling. The listener's brain activity couples with the speaker's, the coupling vanishes when communication fails, and listeners whose brains anticipated the speaker comprehended best. More coupling means better understanding, shared feeling, and stronger memory. Practical translation: clarity is a neurological precondition for connection, and confusion physically decouples your audience.

**Dosed confusion is where learning and engagement live.** Derek Muller's physics education PhD found that clear expository science videos made students more confident without making them more correct. Videos that presented the common misconception first felt confusing to students, and nearly doubled post-test scores. Fifteen years of Veritasium is that finding at scale: attack what the viewer already believes, then rebuild it. Discomfort correlates with both learning and watch time.

**Stories have measurable shapes.** Reagan and colleagues (2016) ran sentiment analysis over 1,300+ Project Gutenberg novels and found six emotional arcs, confirming Vonnegut's rejected thesis. Three arcs dominate the most engaged-with stories: Man in a Hole (fall-rise), Icarus (rise-fall), and Oedipus (fall-rise-fall). Emotion across a script is a plottable curve, which means it is designable in advance.

**Plus the platform physics.** TikTok for Business reports that 63% of the highest click-through videos hook viewers inside the first three seconds. Practitioner benchmarks for short-form: roughly 70% retention at 3 seconds, 60% at 15, and 50% at 30 signal wide-distribution potential, and Hoyos targets 90%+ overall. On long-form, the first 30 seconds is treated across the industry as the strongest single predictor of recommendation. Kahneman's peak-end rule closes the ledger: people judge an experience by its emotional peak and its ending, so the last line carries disproportionate weight in whether they subscribe, share, and come back.

## 2. The five laws

**Law 1: The script starts at the thumbnail.** Packaging is the first line of the script, and the script is the fulfillment of the packaging. Paddy Galloway, the strategist behind MrBeast, Ryan Trahan, and Red Bull, frames it as the thumbnail and title selling the click while the first thirty seconds sell the watch. His data point: top channels spend around 30% of total effort on ideation and packaging while small channels spend around 5%, and outlier videos trace back to idea, title, or thumbnail rather than production upgrades. If you cannot write a clickable package for an idea, the idea is dead. Scrap it before scripting a word.

**Law 2: Borrow deliberately, repay on schedule.** Every hook and open loop is borrowed attention with interest accruing. George Blackman, who scripted for Ali Abdaal at 3M+ subscribers, structures every segment as setup, tension, payoff, and spaces mini-payoffs every 60-90 seconds so the viewer always has something owed to them soon. MrBeast's team is required to know the exact minute mark they are working on at all times, because the retention graph shows precisely where debts went unpaid.

**Law 3: Causality or death.** Trey Parker and Matt Stone's storytelling rule: if the beats of your story connect with "and then," you have a list, and lists bore. Beats must connect with "but" (conflict) or "therefore" (consequence). Jenny Hoyos runs but/therefore inside 34-second Shorts; South Park runs it across 22 minutes. Same physics at every timescale, and it maps cleanly onto Dan Harmon's story circle if you want a full structural template.

**Law 4: Escalate or compress.** MrBeast's leaked production guide calls the first minute the most important of the video, front-loads it with proof that the thumbnail promise is real, then demands what the team calls crazy progression: cover days 1 through 10 in the time an average creator covers day 1. Stakes rise mechanically through his best formats ($1 yacht, $1M yacht, $1B yacht). Trahan's editors apply the same law as a filter: every clip is value delivery, progression, or disruption, and everything else gets cut.

**Law 5: Settle above zero.** Hoyos plans her last line before filming, after watching a single trailing second cost her 20-25 points of retention. The ending is where the payoff lives and where the feeling gets set, so end on the funniest, warmest, or most surprising beat you have, one beat after the promise is paid, then hard stop. Her rule: whatever you say you are going to do, end right after you do it.

## 3. The masters, decoded

**MrBeast: retention engineering in the raw.** The leaked 36-page guide is a scripting document wearing a culture-doc costume. Minute 0-1 must visually prove the thumbnail expectation and front-load stimulation. Minutes 1-3 show rather than tell and compress progression hard. Brand reads get rebuilt as content because the retention graph showed a crater where traditional ad reads sat. Extractable principle: script against the retention graph, minute by minute, with the metrics (CTR, average view duration, average view percentage) as the scoreboard.

**Jenny Hoyos: short-form as lab science.** She scraped and analyzed thousands of Shorts transcripts, including MrBeast's and Trahan's, before designing her own format. Findings: the biggest Shorts read at a first-grade level and she scripts at fifth grade or below; 34 seconds beats sub-30 because sub-30 needs 100%+ retention to go viral; every video is hook, foreshadow, narrative, twist ending; hooks are visual first because visuals process faster than audio. She writes the hook and the last line before filming and fills the middle after. Extractable principle: script for the swipe, in the language of a ten-year-old, with the ending pre-committed.

**Derek Muller / Veritasium: the misconception engine.** His published research ("Saying the wrong thing," 2008) is the only creator playbook that started as peer-reviewed science. Street interviews exist to surface the wrong intuition on camera so the audience feels their own confidence buckle before the rebuild. Extractable principle: open with the thing the viewer wrongly believes, and let cognitive conflict do the retention work.

**Johnny Harris: visual anchor before contextual bridge.** Traditional journalism establishes context first and shows footage as evidence; Harris inverts it. He leads with a concrete visual ("look at this thing"), earns the context after, then alternates anchor and bridge for 20 minutes. He writes the script and the visual direction simultaneously, color-coding assets and fact-checks, and treats every story as a promise made by the title and thumbnail. Extractable principle: anchor with a visual, bridge with context, repeat until resolved.

**Kurzgesagt: research as moat.** Weeks of primary-source reading, multiple experts in the loop, a public sourcesheet per video, and about a dozen script drafts. They name scriptwriting the bottleneck of a 1,200-hour production, and their stated goal is leaving viewers with new insights without the teaching being felt. Extractable principle: research depth is a scripting input, and invisibility of effort is the finish line.

**Ryan Trahan: constraint and character.** A dull premise becomes watchable through constraint: earn the most expensive GTA condo, but legally. Rules get stated up front so tension is legible. Progress is externalized into visible devices (dollars pinned to a wall, the Wheel of Doom), and recurring characters like Kevin the NPC build attachment across a series. Extractable principle: a visible constraint plus a visible scoreboard is a story machine.

**George Blackman: the professionalized system.** Segment-level setup-tension-payoff; educational hooks built from Target, Transformation, Stakes; three scripting depths from bullets to word-for-word depending on time. His sharpest observation: transitions between segments are where viewers leave, so setup and payoff sentences get written in full even when everything else stays bullets. Diagnosis runs on retention graphs rather than headline AVD. Extractable principle: systematize the skeleton, riff the muscle.

**Paddy Galloway: idea supremacy.** Outliers (3-4x channel average) almost never trace to color grades or cameras; they trace to the idea and its packaging. He drafts 30-50 titles per video and runs the glance test: shrink the thumbnail, flash it for a moment, ask whether it parses. Formats transplant across niches, so $1 vs $100 and drag-race structures keep working in fresh contexts. Extractable principle: the ceiling of a video is set before a single line is scripted.

**Alex Hormozi: the content unit.** Hook, retain, reward as the atomic unit; long content is a chain of units; the reward must fully pay the hook's promise. His retention mechanism is the greased chute borrowed from direct response: short sentences, clear flow, zero friction. Extractable principle: trust is the compounding asset, and every unpaid promise is future churn.

## 4. The universal spine

Every high-performing script maps onto the same spine regardless of format.

**Packaging, before any scripting.** One-sentence promise a twelve-year-old could repeat. 30-50 title drafts narrowed to 3. Three genuinely different thumbnail concepts, glance-tested small and fast. The winning package becomes the contract the script must fulfill.

**The hook: 0-30 seconds long-form, 0-3 seconds short-form.** Three jobs in order. First, prove the click was right by visually matching the thumbnail expectation within seconds. Second, sharpen the promise into a specific transformation with stakes: Blackman's educational grammar is Target (this is for you), Transformation (you will walk away with X), Stakes (here is the cost of not knowing). Third, open the first loop with a specific tease rather than a vague one; "you won't believe what happens" is dead weight, while "the third method got me banned" is a debt the viewer will wait to collect. Muller's variant for counterintuitive material: state the misconception as if true, then crack it.

**The body: chains of setup-tension-payoff.** Run a payoff every 60-90 seconds. Each payoff closes one loop and the same breath opens the next: tension, release, setup, tension, release, setup. Write the transition sentences word-for-word even in a bullet script, because transitions are the measured drop-off points. Keep progression visible through a scoreboard, counted steps, or escalating stakes, and on long-form re-anchor the grand payoff every 5-6 minutes.

**The grand payoff.** The single thing they clicked for, delivered at full intensity, never leaked early in diluted form. Blackman's method: identify it first and architect the whole outline backwards from it.

**The ending: the last 5%.** Deliver the peak, settle the account, exit within seconds. CTAs placed before the payoff tax retention; Trahan holds his to one brief mention around a third of the way through, after value has been stacked. The end-screen ask should be the natural next question the video just created.

**Word math.** Educational talking-head runs about 130-150 words per minute spoken; high-energy casual runs up to 200. A 10-minute video is roughly 1,500-1,800 words. A 34-second Short is 80-110. Write to the budget, because overage is where pacing dies.

## 5. Format grammars

**Short-form (TikTok, Reels, Shorts, under 60 seconds).** The swipe decision happens inside 3 seconds and mostly on visuals, so the first frame is the thumbnail. Structure: visual hook (0-2s), spoken expectation plus foreshadow (2-6s), compressed but/therefore narrative (6-28s), twist or punchline in the final second, hard stop. Readability fifth grade or below, one idea only; a second idea is a second video. Dual-track every hook with a text overlay for sound-off viewers and a spoken line for sound-on.

**Talking-head educational (YouTube long-form).** Target-Transformation-Stakes hook inside 30 seconds, then the segment chain with mini-payoffs on the 60-90 second cadence. Pattern interrupts every 30-45 seconds: tone shift, b-roll, location, on-screen text. Use misconception-first openings for anything counterintuitive, and close each segment with a fully written transition sentence.

**Explainer, video essay, documentary.** Harris grammar: visual anchor before contextual bridge, alternating for the full runtime. Frame the piece as a mystery the viewer now needs resolved, and let research depth be the differentiator, because at the top a dozen drafts is normal. The resolution should reframe the opening question and leave one thought running after the video ends.

**Challenge and entertainment.** Constraint stated as explicit rules in the first 30 seconds. Visible scoreboard throughout. Stakes escalate as the format itself, crazy progression through any slow middle, recurring characters and devices for series glue, emotional close. Every clip must be value, progression, or disruption.

**Tutorial and how-to.** Show the finished result in the first 3 seconds, then earn it; this pattern posts some of the strongest 30-second retention in hook studies. Steps are natural mini-payoffs, so name them and count them. For discovery-based content where outcomes are unknown at filming, script only the hook and segment setups beforehand, then write connective voiceover in the edit, which is Blackman's standing advice for review and experiment channels.

**Founder and B2B (LinkedIn-native, 30-90 seconds).** Same physics, different dialect. Open with a contextual hook naming the specific workflow pain in the buyer's vocabulary, and get proof on screen (the product, the number, the artifact) inside 15 seconds. Script for silent viewing with captions and on-screen text, since the large majority of feed video plays muted. Loom-grade authenticity with clean audio outperforms brand-film polish for trust, one idea per video, and the CTA asks for something small before it ever asks for a demo.

## 6. The repeatable process

Run these ten steps in order for every video. Each maps to a law above, which is what makes the output diagnosable rather than lucky.

1. **One-sentence promise.** Write the video as a single sentence a twelve-year-old could repeat back. If it needs a second sentence, it is two videos or zero.
2. **Package first.** 30-50 titles, 3 thumbnail concepts, glance test, outside feedback on the top options. No clickable package means no script.
3. **Lock the grand payoff and the last line.** Hoyos and Blackman converge here from opposite ends of the format spectrum. The ending is a decision, never a leftover.
4. **Loop map.** Outline as a ledger: every loop you open, the minute mark where it closes, and the mini-payoff cadence. Any loop without a scheduled close gets cut or paid.
5. **But/therefore audit on the outline.** Read the beats aloud with connectors spoken. Every "and then" gets rewritten into a but or a therefore, or deleted.
6. **Draft to the word budget.** 150 wpm times target minutes. Transitions and the hook get written word-for-word even if the middle stays bullets.
7. **Hook x10.** Write ten hooks, test the top three against the packaging for promise-match, pick one, and confirm the first 3 seconds work with the sound off.
8. **Trim pass.** Cut anything that is neither value, progression, nor disruption. Then cut 10% more; the Kurzgesagt dozen-draft habit exists because subtraction is the polish.
9. **Read aloud and readability check.** If you stumble, the viewer stumbles. Grade 5 or below for mass audience, grade 8-9 ceiling even for expert B2B.
10. **Ship, then read the graph.** Before writing the next script, review the retention graphs of the last three videos and carry one fix forward. This closes the scientific loop: hypothesis, publish, measure, revise.

## 7. Reading the graph

The retention graph is the instrument that makes this a science, so learn its patterns. Blackman's advice is to review three videos at a time and look for repeating shapes rather than judging any single video.

- **Cliff in the first 30 seconds.** The package promised something the opening did not visually deliver. Fix the hook's promise-match before touching anything else, since mismatched hooks reportedly shed 40%+ of viewers inside 5 seconds.
- **Cliff at one specific timestamp.** An unpaid transition, a segment that opened no loop, or an old-style ad read. Rewrite that transition word-for-word next time, or rebuild the sponsor segment as content the way the MrBeast guide prescribes.
- **Slow steady bleed.** A causality problem rather than a moment problem. Run the but/therefore audit and compress progression harder.
- **Spikes and rewatch bumps.** Those beats are your format telling you what it wants to be. Cluster more disruptions there and consider building whole videos from that beat type.
- **Final-second cliff on Shorts.** You kept talking after the payoff. End on it.
- **Great retention, low views.** A packaging ceiling rather than a script problem. Re-title and re-thumbnail before concluding the idea failed, and sometimes, per Galloway, the right move is changing nothing and waiting.

## 8. The connection layer

Retention makes people watch. Connection makes them come back, share, and buy, and it has mechanics too.

**Readability is respect.** Hasson's coupling research implies that shared language is the precondition for shared feeling; jargon literally decouples brains. Hoyos scripting at fifth grade and MrBeast landing at first grade are connection decisions wearing retention clothes.

**Write to one person.** Hoyos writes for her nieces and her younger self, and checks whether they specifically would stay. A named single viewer produces specificity, and specificity is the texture of truth: "I scraped a thousand transcripts" connects where "I did extensive research" evaporates.

**Make it personal and give it stakes.** Hoyos credits personal goals and irony for investment; Harris hunts paradoxes in human stories; Trahan turns a candy run into an emotional arc by attaching a charity total and a wife who did not see the Airbnb bookings. The Reagan arcs are the design tool here: pick a shape, usually Man in a Hole for a single video, and plot your beats against it.

**Recurring characters and devices.** Kevin the NPC and the Wheel of Doom prove attachment does not require depth, only repetition with warmth. In a personal-brand context, your named frameworks, running bits, and visual motifs do the same job.

**Pay every promise in full.** Hormozi's compounding logic: the reward stage is where the audience decides whether your next hook is credible. Over-deliver and the hook of the following video starts with borrowed trust instead of borrowed skepticism.

**End on the feeling.** Peak-end means the last second is the memory of the whole video. Engineer the final beat for the emotion you want associated with you, then stop.

**Let imperfection read as honesty.** In founder content especially, over-polish creates a trust gap. Clean audio, real screen, human delivery.

---

## 9. Where the science ends

Honest limits, so this stays a philosophy rather than a superstition. Creator advice is survivorship-biased, and the benchmarks above are directional and platform-shifting rather than constants. Hoyos herself suspects shareability matters more than retention orthodoxy admits. Muller's finding cuts against pure smoothness: some friction teaches and holds better than perfect clarity, so optimize for productive discomfort rather than frictionlessness. And the ledger only tells you where a script broke, never what to say; formulas set the floor, taste and a real point of view set the ceiling.

The philosophy compressed to a card: package the promise before you write, open loops with partial information, connect every beat with but or therefore, pay something every 60-90 seconds, escalate or compress, settle the account above zero at the very end, then read the graph and carry one fix into the next script. That is scripting as a science: falsifiable per video, repeatable across videos, and pointed at connection rather than mere watch time.
