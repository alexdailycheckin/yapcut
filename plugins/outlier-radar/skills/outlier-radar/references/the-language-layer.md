# The Language Layer

**A spoken-word spec for video scripts. Companion to Video Scripting as a Science.**

The first document covers structure: what to say and when. This one covers wording: exactly how it should sound. Hand both to a copywriter and they have the architecture and the voice. Compiled July 2026 from broadcast writing doctrine, language psychology research, and transcript analysis of the top creators.

---

## The governing principle

A script is heard once, at speed, by someone doing something else. A reader can re-read; a viewer gets one pass while the content moves past them like a train they cannot stop. Broadcast journalism built its entire writing doctrine on this single fact, and the doctrine holds for every video format.

So every rule below reduces to one test: **can a distracted person understand this sentence the first time they hear it?** If no, rewrite. It is impossible to write too clearly for the ear.

The second principle: write like you talk, to one specific person. The standard broadcast instruction is to write as if telling a friend something interesting, speaking it before you write it. If you would never say the sentence out loud, it does not belong in a script.

---

## Why simple wins: the proof

Simplicity reads as intelligence, and this is measured rather than assumed. Oppenheimer (2006) swapped short words for needlessly long ones in real texts and found readers judged the complex versions as coming from less intelligent, less capable authors, regardless of the underlying quality. The mechanism is processing fluency: whatever is easy to process gets judged as smarter, more likable, and more true.

The creator data agrees from the other direction. Jenny Hoyos scraped thousands of Shorts transcripts and found the biggest hits, including MrBeast's, read at a first-grade level; she scripts her own at fifth grade or below. Hasson's neural coupling research supplies the deepest reason: shared, effortless language is the precondition for brains syncing at all, and every processing stumble is a moment of decoupling.

Conclusion for the copywriter: sophistication lives in the idea, never in the vocabulary. The smartest thing on the page should be the thought.

## Part 1: Words

**Reading level.** Grade 3-5 for mass-audience content, grade 7-8 ceiling for expert B2B. Run every finished script through a readability checker (Hemingway or any Flesch-Kincaid tool) as a hard gate, the way Hoyos does. If the grade is high, the fix is shorter words and shorter sentences rather than dumber ideas.

**Choose the short, common, physical word.** Use, buy, help, need, show, fix. Their formal twins (utilize, purchase, facilitate, require, demonstrate, remediate) cost fluency and buy nothing. Standard substitutions: utilize becomes use, purchase becomes buy, leverage becomes use, implement becomes do or build, obtain becomes get, sufficient becomes enough, in order to becomes to, at this point in time becomes now, individuals becomes people.

**Concrete beats abstract, and it is worth money.** Packard and Berger (2021) found customers were more satisfied and spent more when staff used concrete words: "blue jeans" over "pants," "a coffee" over "anything to drink." In their email study, concrete language preceded customers spending 30% more. The test for any noun or verb: can the viewer picture it? "Grew the channel" is fog; "went from 800 to 40,000 subscribers in nine months" is a picture.

**Certainty beats hedging.** The same research line shows "I can fix that" lands better than "I can try to solve that." Cut maybe, kind of, sort of, I think, hopefully, and try to, unless uncertainty is the actual content. Hedges leak authority one word at a time.

**Numbers: precise in meaning, sayable in sound.** Broadcast rule: the ear handles "nearly 500" better than "four hundred ninety-seven." Exception: when the odd number is the hook ("I built this for $1.37"), keep it, and put it on screen as text so the eye carries the precision. Percentages become fractions when spoken: "about half" beats "51 percent."

**Power words, dosed.** Hoyos leans on words like free, banned, secret, cheap, mistake, and one dollar because they spike attention. One per hook is seasoning; three per paragraph is spam the audience learns to discount.

**Jargon only with instant translation.** First use pays a tax in plain English: "AVD, meaning the minutes people actually watch." After that, the term is shared vocabulary and builds insider feeling. Unexplained jargon decouples; explained jargon bonds.

## Part 2: Sentences

**One idea per sentence.** The listener processes one thought at a time, so stack thoughts vertically instead of nesting them. A long print sentence becomes three or four spoken ones.

**Subject, verb, object.** "The car hit the boy" processes instantly; "the boy was hit by the car" makes the ear reverse-engineer who did what. Active voice, and present tense wherever it is true, because spoken media runs on now.

**Under 20 words, averaging 8-14.** The broadcast ceiling exists for the narrator's breath and the listener's working memory. Sentences that need a comma to survive usually want to be two sentences.

**No mid-sentence clauses.** Constructions with which, that, who, and while ask the listener to hold fact one in suspension while fact two arrives. Split them. "The tool, which we tested for a month, failed" becomes "We tested the tool for a month. It failed."

**Vary the rhythm on purpose.** Two or three medium sentences, then a short one for impact. Fragments are legal in speech. Like this. Monotone sentence length is its own retention leak, whatever the words say.

**Put the money word last.** The end of a sentence is the stress position; the ear treats the final word as the point. "This mistake cost me forty thousand dollars" hits; "forty thousand dollars is what this mistake cost me" wilts. Punchlines, reveals, and numbers go last.

**Talk like a person.** Contractions always: it's, you're, don't. Starting sentences with And, But, or So is correct spoken grammar and doubles as audible but/therefore connective tissue.

**Attribute before the claim.** Spoken information needs the source first: "MrBeast's internal guide says the first minute decides the video." The reversed print order ("The first minute decides the video, according to...") leaves the listener holding an unclaimed fact.

## Part 3: Person grammar, the relationship encoder

Pronouns are where relationship lives in language, and they are measurable.

**"You" is the main character.** Packard and Berger (2020) analyzed 4,200 Billboard-charting songs and found lyrics with more second-person pronouns were liked and bought more. The mechanism matters: "you" works because it makes the audience think about their own life, and the effect was strongest when "you" is the object of the action ("this keeps happening to you") rather than only the subject. Mayer's personalization research adds that people learn better from conversational, you-based language than formal language. Practical rule: in educational content, "you" should outnumber "I," and every generalization should be convertible into a you-sentence.

**"I" is for ownership and confession.** Research on service language found "I" statements beat corporate "we" for satisfaction because they signal personal agency: someone specific is on the hook. Use "I" to own mistakes, stakes, and receipts: "I lost the deal. Here is the email I should have sent."

**"We" only when it is true.** Reserve it for genuinely shared experience, especially the shared-enemy move: "We've all sat through that meeting." Corporate "we" ("here at the channel we believe") is distance wearing a smile.

**Write to one person, by name, in your head.** Hoyos writes for her nieces and her younger self, and it shows in every word choice. Address forms follow: "you," singular, always. Never "hey guys," "some of you," "those of you who," or "everyone watching," because each one tells the viewer they are a crowd.

## Part 4: Connective tissue, the spoken but/therefore lexicon

The first document made transitions load-bearing: they are the measured drop-off points, so they get written word-for-word. This is the phrase bank that keeps causality audible. Every connector points forward and none exceeds one short line.

- **But-family (conflict):** "But here's the problem." / "Except it didn't work." / "The catch?" / "Then it broke." / "Here's where it gets weird."
- **Therefore-family (consequence):** "So here's what I did." / "Which means..." / "That changes everything, because..." / "Turns out..."
- **Setup-family (opening the next loop):** "And that's when I found the thing that actually matters." / "Which brings up the real question..." / "There's one more problem, and it's the big one."

Banned connectors: "and then" (a list, ergo boredom), "moving on," "next up," "anyway," and any sentence that looks backward ("so as we discussed earlier") instead of forward.

---

## Part 5: Loop language, how curiosity is phrased

Loewenstein's finding sets the formula: curiosity fires on a partial dose of information, so a tease must be specific and incomplete at the same time. The pattern is **concrete noun plus withheld outcome.**

Weak, because fully vague: "You won't believe what happens next." Strong, because half-revealed: "The third method got me banned." Hoyos's version of the rule: open loops must never feel vague, so tease with specifics without giving the resolution, and keep any foreshadow line under three spoken seconds.

Question hooks are allowed only when the viewer would genuinely ask the question themselves. Muller's misconception grammar is the strongest opener in the educational arsenal: state the wrong belief in the viewer's own words, as if true, then crack it. "Heavier objects fall faster. Obviously. Except they don't, and the reason rewired physics."

## Part 6: Relatability moves

These are the wording patterns that build relationship rather than just hold attention.

**The confession formula: belief, cost, correction.** "I thought posting daily was the answer. It cost me a year. I was wrong." Self-disclosure with a price tag reads as honesty; self-disclosure without one reads as content.

**Voice the viewer's thought before they think it.** "You're probably thinking this only works with a big audience. Fair. Here's the version for zero followers." Naming the objection out loud is the audible form of listening, and concreteness research shows signaled listening is what drives the satisfaction effect.

**Name the feeling before the fact.** "This is the part everyone dreads." / "This number should scare you a little." Emotion labels give the viewer a place to stand while information arrives.

**Callbacks and coined phrases.** Reusing a named idea, running joke, or recurring character builds the attachment Trahan gets from Kevin the NPC and the Wheel of Doom. In a personal-brand context, your named frameworks are the recurring characters, so say them the same way every time.

**Scripted imperfection.** A self-correction ("wait, that's underselling it"), a short aside, a laugh at your own line. Micro-flaws read as a human being; unbroken polish reads as an ad. This is dosage work: one or two per video, written in, never rambled in.

**Respect as a register.** Broadcast doctrine again: never talk down. The target register is smart friend explaining to a smart, busy friend, and it rules out both lecture-voice and hype-voice.

## Part 7: Rhythm and delivery markup

Scripts are sheet music, so mark the performance.

Pace: 130-150 words per minute for educational delivery, up to 200 for high energy, roughly 2.5-3 words per second on Shorts. Write to the budget. Mark one emphasis word per key sentence in CAPS, insert (beat) where a pause does the work of a sentence, and break lines where the breath falls. Then apply the oldest rule in radio: read it aloud, and every stumble is a rewrite order, because if the mouth trips, the ear already fell.

## Part 8: The banned list

Throat-clearing openers, delete on sight: "In this video I'm going to..." / "Welcome back to the channel." / "Before we start..." / "Without further ado." / "Let's dive in." The video starts at word one.

Corporate and AI-flavored vocabulary: leverage, utilize, delve, seamless, robust, streamline, elevate, unlock, empower, game-changer, revolutionize, cutting-edge, "in today's fast-paced world." Each one is a fluency tax and an authenticity tell.

Vague intensifiers: very, really, actually, basically, literally, incredibly, super. They add syllables and subtract force; a precise noun or number does their job.

Empty teases: "stay tuned," "wait for it," "you won't believe," "trust me." Every tease must carry a concrete noun or it is spam.

Crowd address and hype: "guys, listen up," "smash that like button" cold, superlatives the payoff cannot cash. Unpaid superlatives are the fastest way to teach an audience to discount your hooks.

## Part 9: The dialect dial by format

**Short-form.** Grade 5 or below, sentences of 2-10 words, every noun visualizable, power word in the first line, text overlay carrying the hook for sound-off viewers. No word exists that does not survive a swipe decision.

**Long-form educational.** Grade 5-7. Warmer, more asides and confessions, jargon allowed after translation, emphasis and (beat) marks throughout, you-heavy throughout with "I" reserved for receipts and mistakes.

**Founder and B2B.** Grade 7-8 ceiling, and the discipline flips in one place: use the ICP's exact vocabulary for the pain ("pipeline coverage," "handoff between SDR and AE") and plain English for everything else. Numbers carry the authority, hedges stay banned, and certainty phrasing ("this works, here's the proof") does the persuading. The register is a sharp operator talking to a peer, never a brand talking to a segment.

## Part 10: Rewrites, before and after

**Opener, educational.**
Before: "In this video, we're going to be discussing some strategies for optimizing your content's performance on social platforms."
After: "Your videos are dying in the first 30 seconds. I can show you the exact second mine stopped dying."

**Product, B2B.**
Before: "Our solution leverages AI to streamline your reporting workflows and unlock team productivity."
After: "You paste the call transcript. It writes the QBR deck. Your Monday just got two hours back."

**Tease.**
Before: "Stick around, because later I'll share something you won't believe."
After: "At the end, I'll show the one metric that predicted every flop, including this one."

**Claim.**
Before: "Utilizing concrete terminology has been demonstrated to significantly enhance customer satisfaction outcomes."
After: "Say 'blue jeans' instead of 'pants' and customers spend more. Researchers measured it: 30% more."

**Confession.**
Before: "Many creators struggle with consistency in the early stages of their journey."
After: "I posted 62 videos before anything worked. Number 63 is why you're watching this."

**Transition.**
Before: "Moving on to the next point, let's now talk about thumbnails."
After: "But none of that matters if nobody clicks. Which is a thumbnail problem, and mine were terrible."

## Part 11: The copywriter handoff card

Fill this in once per creator or brand, attach it to every brief, and a competent copywriter can write in-voice from day one.

1. **Persona line.** "Write as [name], a [two-trait description], talking to one person: [named viewer, their situation]." Example: "a blunt operator who shows receipts, talking to a Series B head of growth who is smart, skeptical, and out of time."
2. **Register and reading level.** Target grade, checked in a named tool, plus the energy setting (calm authority, playful, urgent) and words-per-minute budget for the format.
3. **Pronoun policy.** You-first, "I" for stakes and receipts, "we" only for shared enemies, singular address always.
4. **Ten signature phrases.** The coined frameworks, recurring bits, and connective lines that must appear in-voice, spelled exactly.
5. **Ten banned words.** The generic list above plus anything off-brand for this voice.
6. **One sample paragraph.** Fifty words of perfect in-voice writing as the tuning fork.
7. **QA gate, run in order.** Read aloud with zero stumbles; grade level passes; longest sentence under 20 words; hedge scan clean; but/therefore audible at every transition; every tease carries a concrete noun; every claim carries a number or a picture; final word of key sentences is the money word.
